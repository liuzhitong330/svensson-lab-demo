The Svensson Lab’s peptide-discovery work raises a question beyond BRP: what do the other predicted peptides do, and in which settings should we look for them? For the lab’s GLP-1 and cancer research, I wanted to make that next choice more concrete.

I built a candidate explorer that connects the lab’s peptide predictions to public pancreatic-lesion RNA data from a semaglutide study. It asks which precursors deserve peptide detection and cellular follow-up—not which peptides have already been shown to affect cancer.

How it works

Start with a candidate, inspect the individual animals, then compare the human and mouse peptide sequences and predicted cleavage sites. Change the evidence requirements to see which candidates remain on a practical follow-up shortlist.

RNA data: external Cencioni study, GSE266899, late time point. Eleven male mice: LFD 4, HFD 4, HFD + semaglutide 3. Samples are pancreatic neoplastic lesions, not uniformly established carcinoma; LFD is not a healthy baseline. Semaglutide began before engraftment.

What result would change the next step?

If a correctly identified endogenous peptide is detected, test the matched mouse sequence with a dose range, vehicle, sequence-control and viability readouts. If precursor RNA is present but the peptide is not detected, investigate processing and producing cells before treating a synthetic-peptide response as physiological.

A positive cellular result would motivate measurements in an independently designed semaglutide cancer experiment, with tumor, host and exposure timing recorded separately. It would not establish that the peptide mediates semaglutide’s effect.

Why this matters for the lab

The lab’s discovery platform makes it possible to ask which overlooked peptide signals deserve an experiment. Here, the handoff is a small set of precursor–sequence pairs, their evidence gaps and the next measurement for each—connecting the interpretation of an animal study to a tractable cellular follow-up.

Methods note

This is an exploratory cross-dataset analysis of precursor RNA and predicted sequences. Bulk RNA does not measure peptide abundance, cleavage, secretion, cell of origin or causal cancer activity. Cellular composition and host physiology are alternative explanations; no receptor, activity or therapeutic effect is inferred.

Normalization, selection rules and limitations

The small late-time-point cohort is analyzed separately from earlier samples. Leave-one-animal-out checks diagnose dependence on a single observation; they do not provide inferential confidence intervals. Thresholds are transparent prioritization choices, not biological cutoffs or a significance test.

Human–mouse sequence correspondence and a predicted cleavage motif are evidence for choosing an assay sequence, not proof that the endogenous peptide is released. Apparent conservation does not establish conserved function.

These are fragments from the published prediction library, which includes membrane and adhesion proteins as well as soluble proteins. A known function of the full-length precursor is not evidence that its predicted short fragment has that function.

This is our proposed extension of the public peptide-discovery work, not an identified internal lab project. Analysis and implementation were assistant-supported. The project does not establish prior hands-on peptidomics or GLP-1 assay experience.
